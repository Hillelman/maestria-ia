Neurological disorders are difficult to detect early, to stage consistently, and to monitor objectively. Clinical assessment is still indispensable, but it is also inherently variable and often insensitive during presymptomatic or mildly symptomatic phases [1], [2], [3], [4]. Eye movements are especially attractive as candidate biomarkers because they reflect the coordinated activity of cortical, cerebellar, basal-ganglia, and brainstem circuits. As a result, abnormalities in saccades, antisaccades, smooth pursuit, fixation, and microsaccades can reveal both motor and cognitive dysfunction [2], [5], [6]. The papers supplied for this survey all converge on one idea: computer vision can make oculomotor assessment far more accessible by replacing dedicated infrared eye trackers with ordinary RGB cameras. That focus is narrower than “computer vision for neurology” in general, but it is also strategically important. Compared with MRI or PET, camera-based assessment is inexpensive, portable, repeatable, and suitable for home use. The literature now spans the full pipeline from gaze-estimation methodology [7], [8], [9] to clinical-grade video oculometrics [10] and disease prediction [11]. This survey makes three claims. First, the field’s real breakthrough is democratization of measurement rather than replacement of neurological expertise. Second, the highest-value clinical use case today is structured screening and longitudinal monitoring, especially for PD, rather than autonomous diagnosis across heterogeneous neurological disorders. Third, the main bottleneck is no longer whether gaze can be estimated from video, but whether learned biomarkers generalize across sites, populations, devices, medications, and task designs. The literature review below develops these claims as a cohesive story from clinical rationale, to algorithmic evolution, to translational evidence and unresolved limitations.

A. Why eye movements are informative neurological biomarkers
The biological motivation for camera-based eye tracking is unusually strong. Anderson and MacAskill showed that eye movements provide a window into distributed neural systems and can support differential diagnosis across age-related neurodegenerative disorders [2]. Antoniades and Kennard similarly argued that ocular motor abnormalities are among the most informative clues available to clinicians because gaze-holding, saccadic control, vergence, and pursuit each map onto partially dissociable pathways [3]. Leigh and Kennard further noted that saccades are experimentally attractive because they are rapid, precisely measurable, and tied to well-characterized neural mechanisms [6].
This clinical foundation matters because computer vision systems are only as useful as the signal they seek to capture. The literature suggests that the oculomotor phenotype is meaningful across several disorders, but not in identical ways. In PD, voluntary saccades are often more affected than reflexive ones, with hypometria, increased latency, impaired inhibition, altered pursuit, and convergence deficits recurring across studies [5], [12]. In AD and mild cognitive impairment, systematic evidence links disease to abnormal saccade latency, inhibitory control, and microsaccadic behavior [13], [14]. Garbutt et al. demonstrated that oculomotor behavior can also help separate frontotemporal lobar degeneration from Alzheimer’s disease and related syndromes [15]. Band et al. reviewed similar evidence for PD, AD, and ALS, emphasizing that some oculometric measures correlate with established clinical scales such as MDS-UPDRS, ALSFRS-R, and MMSE [1].
The significance of this literature is twofold. It supports the premise that eye movements are neurologically relevant, and it also reveals why simplistic disease classifiers are risky. Oculomotor abnormalities are often overlapping rather than disease-specific, and their magnitude depends on task design, medication state, fatigue, visual acuity, and comorbid ophthalmic factors [3], [13]. Therefore, computer vision systems should not be evaluated only as pattern recognizers; they should also be judged by whether the features they recover remain clinically interpretable under realistic variation.

B. From specialized hardware to unconstrained computer vision
Historically, accurate eye-movement measurement depended on electrooculography, scleral search coils, or infrared eye trackers, all of which introduced either invasiveness, operator burden, or deployment cost [1]. The first major contribution of computer vision was to relax these hardware constraints. Sugano et al. proposed an incremental appearance-based gaze estimator that learned from ordinary desktop interaction, used pose-based clustering to tolerate head movement, and avoided a dedicated calibration stage [7]. Its weighted error remained around 4–5 degrees, which was not sufficient for fine oculometrics, but the paper was important because it reframed gaze tracking as a software problem solvable with weak labels and minimal equipment. Krafka et al. made the next pivotal move by coupling deep learning with scale [8]. Their GazeCapture dataset contained data from 1,474 subjects and about 2.45 million frames, and the iTracker architecture combined crops of both eyes, the face, and a face-grid representation of head location. Reported errors of 1.71 cm on phones and 2.53 cm on tablets without calibration, with real-time inference at roughly 10–15 fps on mobile hardware, showed that commodity devices could support robust gaze estimation outside carefully controlled laboratories [8]. The impact of this paper extends beyond its metric values: it established dataset scale and representation learning as the primary drivers of practical gaze estimation. Yet Krafka et al. also exposed a key limitation that is easy to miss when the field is discussed only in engineering terms. Screen-point error in centimeters is a useful metric for human-computer interaction, but neurological assessment often depends on angular precision, timing, and stability at a much finer scale. A gaze tracker that is excellent for interface control may still be inadequate for estimating saccadic latency or microsaccades. That gap helps explain why clinical translation lagged behind consumer gaze estimation. Recent deep models address part of this gap by integrating additional structure. Hu and Huang proposed multitask frameworks that estimate both gaze direction and gaze point, introduce binocular focus constraints, and use attention mechanisms to force the network to preserve eye-relevant information [9]. Their work is significant because it treats gaze as a geometrically constrained problem rather than a purely discriminative regression task. In principle, this is attractive for clinical settings where depth ambiguity, occlusion, and cross-eye inconsistency can corrupt downstream biomarkers. However, their evaluation remains benchmark-centered rather than disease-centered, and the authors acknowledge sensitivity to eye visibility, facial resolution, and computational cost [9]. The broader lesson is that better gaze models are necessary, but not sufficient, for clinical usefulness.

C. Toward clinical-grade RGB oculometrics
Harpaz et al. provide one of the clearest demonstrations that webcam-style video can cross from generic gaze estimation into clinically relevant oculometrics [10]. Their NeuraLight Gaze Estimation Model (NLGEM), combined with an additional Calibrated Gaze Model (CGM), used stimulus target locations for ongoing real-time calibration and was validated against an infrared tracker in 259 participants performing pro-saccade tasks. The combined system achieved 1.80◦ absolute mean error, with axial errors of 0.93◦ horizontally and 1.31◦ vertically [10]. These values are not just engineering improvements; they move RGB video closer to the spatial accuracy required for clinically meaningful oculometric measures.
This paper matters because it shifts the role of computer vision from “approximate attention estimation” to “measurement infrastructure.” Band et al. make this point explicitly in their review, arguing that portable, patient-friendly video oculography could support remote home monitoring, larger clinical datasets, and lower-cost pharmaceutical trials [1]. In that sense, computer vision has systemic impact: it can enlarge the population that is measurable, not only marginally improve accuracy on the population already reachable with clinical hardware.
At the same time, the Harpaz and Band papers invite caution. Both are closely linked to the same company, and Band et al. explicitly disclose industry employment and funding [1]. Industry-led work is not inherently weaker, but it increases the need for independent replication, particularly when a paper is simultaneously describing a technical advance and a commercial deployment vision. A second limitation is methodological: self-calibration with known stimulus locations is powerful, but it also means performance may depend on structured task design. Free-viewing or passive monitoring remains more difficult because the system cannot rely on externally known targets.

D. From measurement to disease prediction
The field becomes clinically ambitious when oculometrics are no longer the endpoint but the input to disease prediction. Dedicated eye-tracker studies already showed that this is plausible. Brien et al. used video-based eye tracking and machine learning to classify and stage PD, obtaining an ROC-AUC of 0.88, 83% sensitivity, and 78% specificity from saccade, pupil, and blink features [16]. Li et al. pushed performance further by combining gait and eye-movement features, reporting AUC values above 0.95 in both training and validation sets for PD diagnosis [17]. These studies are important because they show that ocular behavior contains clinically predictive information, but they remain tied to specialized sensors or multimodal setups that limit scalability.
Webcam-based disease prediction is newer and less mature. Harisinghani et al. showed that deep models trained on webcam gaze data can extract predictive signal for Alzheimer’s classification during a reading task, though they still underperformed systems based on high-end eye trackers [18]. This is exactly where the uploaded literature becomes most interesting: it suggests that the quality of the upstream gaze model may determine whether a clinical classifier is merely feasible or truly competitive.
Gao et al. are the strongest current example [11]. Their pipeline, illustrated in Fig. 1 of the paper, extracts gaze and bilateral pupil trajectories from two ordinary RGB videos using DeepPupil and MCGaze, computes clinically meaningful features such as pursuit gain, saccade amplitude, and latency, and then feeds both raw time series and handcrafted features into a transformer-style anomaly-attention model. On an imbalanced cohort of 129 participants (103 PD, 26 healthy controls), standard machine learning on handcrafted features reached 0.74 balanced accuracy, a CNN reached 0.79, and anomaly attention reached 0.88 when clinical features were included [11]. The model also assigned higher attention to later, more difficult cycles of the tracking task for PD participants, which is valuable because it ties predictive performance back to task structure rather than offering only a black-box score [11].
The significance of Gao et al. is substantial. It shows that regular RGB video can do more than approximate clinical hardware; in a structured PD task, it can support a deep model that surpasses a cited specialized-equipment baseline from prior work [11]. The impact is equally clear: if the result generalizes, early PD screening could move from specialist labs to commodity devices. However, the limitations are just as important as the headline number. The cohort is small and single-site. The class distribution is heavily imbalanced. The task is highly structured, with explicit head stabilization and repeated cyclic motion. The pipeline is modular, so errors in face detection, pupil localization, and gaze estimation can accumulate before classification. The authors openly note these weaknesses and propose future integration of the modules into a unified model [11]. For that reason, the study should be interpreted as proof of promise for scalable screening, not as evidence that commodity video is ready to replace clinical diagnosis.

E. Critical synthesis
Taken together, the literature suggests a two-step value chain. First, computer vision democratizes measurement by turning commodity RGB cameras into approximate eye trackers. Second, machine learning converts those oculometrics into disease-relevant predictions. The first step is already well supported. Foundational gaze-estimation work showed feasibility under unconstrained conditions [7], [8]; newer multitask and self-calibrating methods improved geometric consistency and angular accuracy [9], [10]. The second step is promising but less mature. Disease classifiers can work well in constrained protocols, especially for PD [16], [11], [17], but evidence across disorders and deployment contexts is still thin. This distinction matters for significance and impact. The biggest immediate contribution of computer vision is likely not autonomous diagnosis, but software-defined biomarkers that can be collected frequently, remotely, and at scale. That could improve clinical trials, enable progression tracking, and make specialist assessment available to geographically dispersed patients [1]. The main limitations are equally systemic: demographic bias, site-specific protocols, medication effects, visual confounds such as glasses and ptosis, and the possibility that models learn nuisance cues such as head pose regularization or facial rigidity instead of clean oculomotor physiology. In short, the field is moving in the right direction, but its strongest claims still rest on relatively narrow data.

F. Future Directions
Future work should prioritize end-to-end clinical learning. Most current systems separate face processing, pupil detection, gaze estimation, feature extraction, and disease classification. This modularity is convenient, but it propagates upstream errors and hides uncertainty. Joint optimization could let disease supervision influence which visual features are preserved while also providing calibrated confidence estimates for clinicians.
A second priority is multi-site, longitudinal validation. Most studies remain small, single-center, and cross-sectional [11], [10]. Robust deployment will require standardized metadata on medication state, ophthalmic history, device type, ambient illumination, and task compliance, together with repeated measurements over time. Without that, strong within-study results may simply reflect site- or protocol-specific regularities.
Third, the field should move toward multimodal and ecologically valid assessment. Eye movements alone are informative, but Li et al. already showed that combining gaze with gait improves PD diagnosis [17]. Video-based facial expressivity, speech, posture, and free-living behavior are natural extensions. AR/VR and passive smartphone monitoring may also broaden the observable task space, but only if calibration-free methods become reliable enough for free-viewing conditions [1], [9].
Finally, deployment requires clinical interpretability, fairness, and privacy. Attention maps, uncertainty estimates, and physiologically meaningful features should be used to audit what the model is actually learning, particularly across age, ethnicity, eyewear, and disease severity. Privacy-preserving feature sharing is also essential because raw facial video is sensitive. Gao et al. provide processed time-series data and code rather than raw videos, which is one reasonable compromise [11].

Conclusion
Computer vision can help predict and diagnose neurological disorders most convincingly when it is used to recover oculomotor biomarkers from ordinary video. The literature reviewed here shows a clear progression: early appearance-based methods made unconstrained gaze estimation feasible, large-scale deep learning made it practical, self-calibrating and multitask models made it more precise, and recent PD studies showed that structured RGB-video assessments can support clinically relevant screening performance. The work is significant because it lowers cost, increases reach, and enables repeated measurement. Its impact could be substantial for remote monitoring and early screening. Its main limitation is that diagnosis remains downstream of small, structured, and only partially validated datasets. The near-term future of this field is therefore not “camera-only neurology,” but clinically grounded computer vision systems that provide scalable, interpretable, and longitudinal digital biomarkers.
